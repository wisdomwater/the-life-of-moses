## Preface

Some books find us exactly when we need them.

For me, Gregory of Nyssa was one of those discoveries.

I had heard his name before, usually in conversations about the early Church Fathers and, more specifically, about the hopeful vision of salvation that has often been called a form of Christian universalism. That alone caught my attention. Here was a fourth-century bishop who seemed to speak of God's love with a breadth and confidence that felt both ancient and surprisingly fresh. Yet, beyond that, I knew very little about him.

Then I learned that Gregory had written *The Life of Moses*.

At first, I assumed it would be a commentary on the biblical story. Instead, I discovered something far richer. Gregory reads the life of Moses not merely as history but as a map of the soul. Egypt becomes our bondage to the false self. The Red Sea becomes our passage into freedom. The wilderness becomes the place where God patiently reshapes us. Sinai becomes the ascent of prayer. Even the darkness where Moses encounters God becomes a symbol of the mystery into which every believer is invited.

The more I read, the more I realized that Gregory was not simply explaining Moses. He was describing the Christian life itself.

What surprised me most was how modern his insights felt. Though separated from us by more than sixteen centuries, Gregory understood the struggles of the human heart with remarkable clarity. He knew how easily we become distracted, how often pride follows victory, how desire can disguise itself as freedom, and how God patiently draws us beyond every stage of growth into something deeper. Again and again, I found myself thinking, *This could have been written for today's believer.*

Gregory of Nyssa lived approximately from A.D. 335 to 395. Alongside his brother, Basil the Great, and his friend, Gregory of Nazianzus, he is remembered as one of the Cappadocian Fathers, whose writings helped shape the Church's understanding of the Trinity during one of Christianity's most formative periods. He served as Bishop of Nyssa in Asia Minor and became one of the greatest mystical theologians in Christian history.

*The Life of Moses* was written late in Gregory's life, likely in the 390s, after decades of pastoral ministry and theological reflection. It was addressed to a young believer named Caesarius, who had asked what a life of true spiritual perfection looked like. Gregory's answer is as beautiful as it is unexpected. Perfection, he says, is not arriving at a final destination. It is an endless journey into the infinite goodness of God. Because God is inexhaustible, there is always more love to receive, more beauty to behold, and more of Christ into whom we may be transformed.

That single idea has profoundly shaped Christian spirituality. Gregory's vision echoes through later contemplatives such as Maximus the Confessor, Pseudo-Dionysius, Symeon the New Theologian, and countless others in both the Eastern and Western traditions. His conviction that the soul forever grows into God—what he called *epektasis*, the continual stretching forward—remains one of the most compelling visions of the Christian life ever written.

This devotional is not a translation of Gregory's work, nor is it an academic commentary. Rather, it is an attempt to let his spiritual vision speak in a warm, contemporary voice. Each reflection seeks to preserve the heart of Gregory's teaching while expressing it in language that invites modern readers into prayer, contemplation, and transformation.

As I worked through these pages, I came to appreciate Gregory for more than his hopeful theology. I came to admire him as a wise spiritual companion. Again and again, he gently redirected my attention away from merely becoming more religious and toward becoming more fully alive in Christ. He reminded me that the Christian life is not about mastering spiritual techniques or accumulating theological knowledge. It is about learning to love the God whose love has no end.

My hope is that these seventy-five meditations become more than daily readings. I hope they become quiet companions on your own journey. Whether you are just leaving Egypt or have been walking with Christ for many years, may you find yourself encouraged to take one more step up the mountain. May you discover that every ascent reveals another horizon, every prayer opens another mystery, and every glimpse of Christ awakens a deeper longing for Him.

Most of all, may you come away with the same realization that Gregory spent his life proclaiming: that the end of the spiritual journey is not an ending at all, but an everlasting beginning in the infinite love of God.
